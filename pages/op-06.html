Can We Train Neural Networks to Preserve Useful Topology?

November 2026 7 min read Status: Open
Sunjun Hwang
Independent Researcher, Vancouver · Quantum Computing, AI Security & Robust ML
CV Research Scholar Email

In one sentence

Instead of only measuring the shape of a network's internal representation after training, can we build the desired shape into training itself — so the network learns, for example, one clean piece per class and no spurious holes — and does that make it better?

What is the problem?

Imagine training a network to outline blood vessels in retinal scans. The result scores 98% on a pixel-by-pixel measure. But when a doctor looks at it, a single vessel has been broken into three disconnected pieces, and two separate vessels have been fused into one. Almost every pixel is right; the shape is wrong — and for the doctor, the shape is what matters.

The same thing happens inside networks that do not output shapes at all. A classifier's representation might split one class into several islands, or let two classes touch at a thin bridge. Accuracy on the training set can still be high, because the loss function — the number training tries to make small — only checks whether each answer is right. It says nothing about how the representation is organised.

So far on this list, topology has been a diagnostic: something we compute to understand a model (OP-05) or to warn about failure (OP-04). This open problem asks whether topology can be a design goal: added to the loss or used as a regulariser, so the network is directly pushed toward a representation with the shape we want.

Why does it matter?

  • Correct shape, not just correct pixels. In tasks like segmenting vessels, roads, neurons or cell membranes, connectivity errors are what users actually notice. A topology-aware loss attacks those errors directly.
  • Robustness by construction. If robust representations have a particular shape (OP-02), training for that shape might give robustness without training against any specific attack (OP-03).
  • Faithful compressed views. When high-dimensional data is squeezed into a few dimensions for visualisation or compression, preserving its clusters and loops keeps the picture honest.
  • Built-in monitoring. A network trained to have a known shape gives a clear reference: if deployed data breaks that shape, something is wrong.

What would a solution look like?

The mechanism is a second term in the loss. The usual training loop compares the network's answers with the labels and nudges the weights to reduce the error. The topological version adds a parallel path that measures the shape of the representation and penalises the difference from a target shape.

What "target shape" means depends on the goal:

GoalWhat the topology loss asks for
Clean classesEach class forms a single connected piece, well separated from the others.
Correct structure in outputsA predicted segmentation has the same number of pieces and holes as the true one.
Faithful compressionThe compressed representation keeps the clusters and loops of the original data.
Simple decision bordersThe regions the classifier assigns to each class have no unnecessary islands or holes.

The key idea: making shape something a network can learn from

Networks learn by following gradients — small signals that say, for each weight, "move a bit this way to reduce the loss." For that to work, every step from the weights to the loss must respond smoothly to small changes.

Topology looks like the opposite of smooth. The number of pieces or holes is a whole number; it jumps from 2 to 1 at a single moment. That seems useless for gradients. The insight that makes this problem possible is that a persistence barcode is not just a count — each bar has a birth and a death value, and each of those is set by a specific pair of points. Moving those points moves the bar's ends continuously. So we can say "make this bar shorter" (merge these pieces sooner) or "make this bar longer" (keep this hole open), and translate that into a push on particular points, and from there into a push on the weights.

That turns topology from something we observe into something we can steer — at least locally, and at least in principle.

Why is it hard?

  • Cost per step. Persistent homology has to be computed at every training step, on every batch. That can easily dominate training time.
  • Sparse, jumpy gradients. Each bar depends on only a few points, so most points get no signal at all, and the set of "responsible" points can change abruptly from one step to the next. Training can become unstable.
  • Batches see only part of the shape. A batch of 128 points is a small, random glimpse of the whole representation. The shape of the batch may not reflect the shape of the data.
  • Which shape is "useful"? For segmentation the target is clear. For a classifier's internal representation, it is not obvious which topology to ask for — and asking for the wrong one could hurt (see OP-05).
  • Trade-off with accuracy. The topology term competes with the task term. Too little weight and nothing changes; too much and accuracy suffers.

What has been tried so far

  • Topological losses for segmentation — comparing the barcodes of predicted and true masks, and penalising extra or missing pieces and holes. Clear improvements in getting thin, connected structures right.
  • Topological regularisers for classifiers — penalising unnecessary pieces in the decision regions, so the classifier draws simpler borders.
  • Topological autoencoders — adding a loss that keeps the topology of the compressed representation close to that of the input data.
  • Shaping representation connectivity — training representations so that their pieces connect at a chosen scale, which helps later tasks that rely on that structure.
  • General tools — differentiable topology layers that can be dropped into any network, and theory on when gradient-based optimisation of persistence-based functions actually converges.

Most of this work targets the output (segmentation masks, decision regions) or a compressed copy of the data. Much less is known about shaping the hidden representation of a standard classifier, and whether doing so makes it more robust or easier to monitor.

How I would approach it

  1. Start with the simplest target: ask a classifier's middle-layer representation to form exactly one connected piece per class within a batch, with nothing else changed.
  2. Compare it with an ordinary model and with a simpler, non-topological regulariser that pulls classes together, to see whether topology adds anything beyond "make clusters tighter".
  3. Test the trained models on corruptions and attacks they never saw (OP-03), and on the early-warning setup from OP-04 — does a network trained to have a clean shape give a clearer alarm when that shape breaks?
  4. Track the cost honestly: training time, stability across random seeds, and sensitivity to the weight given to the topology term.

Questions still open

  1. Which target topology actually helps a classifier — and is it the same for different datasets and architectures?
  2. Does training for a clean representation shape improve robustness on its own, or only when combined with other methods? (see OP-02, OP-03)
  3. Can topological losses be made cheap and stable enough for large models and large batches?
  4. How should a topology computed on small batches be trusted as a guide to the shape of the whole dataset?
  5. Does a network with a deliberately shaped representation make failures easier to detect? (see OP-01, OP-04)
  6. In federated learning, could each client's training include a shared topological target, so that clients with very different data still learn compatible representations? (see OP-10)

This is the "engineering" end of the topology questions on this list: OP-04 asks whether topology can warn us, OP-05 asks what it means, and this problem asks whether we can put it to work during training.
