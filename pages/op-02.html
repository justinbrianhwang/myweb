What Makes a Representation Inherently Robust?

November 2026 7 min read Status: Open
Sunjun Hwang
Independent Researcher, Vancouver · Quantum Computing, AI Security & Robust ML
CV Research Scholar Email

In one sentence

When two networks are equally accurate but one shrugs off small disturbances and the other falls apart, what is different about the inside of the robust one — and can we name that property directly, instead of only measuring the end result?

What is the problem?

Take two image classifiers trained on the same photos. Both score 95% on the test set. Now add a layer of noise to each photo — so faint that a person cannot see it. The first model still gets almost everything right. The second one starts calling cats "trucks".

From the outside, before the noise, these two models looked identical. Same accuracy, same kind of architecture, same data. Something about how each one organized what it learned must be different, and that difference only shows up when the input is disturbed.

Every network turns an input into an internal description — a long list of numbers called its representation. The final answer is read off that description. So the natural place to look for the difference is there: in the shape of the space where the representations live.

Today we usually summarize robustness with two numbers: accuracy on clean data and robust accuracy under a chosen attack. Those numbers tell us how much a model resists. They do not tell us why. This open problem asks for the why: a property of the representation itself — its geometry or its topology — that explains robustness rather than just recording it.

Why does it matter?

If robustness can only be measured by attacking a model, then every new model has to be attacked again, with every attack we can think of, and we still never know what we missed. A property we could read off the representation would change that:

  • we could check a model's robustness without running an expensive battery of attacks,
  • we could train for that property directly, rather than training against one particular attack (see OP-03),
  • we could compare architectures and training recipes by what they do to the representation, not only by a leaderboard number,
  • and it would give us an actual explanation — something that tells us where robustness comes from, not just that it is there.

What would a solution look like?

Imagine the representation space as a map. Each input lands somewhere on it, and inputs of the same class tend to gather into clusters. The model's decision is essentially "which region of the map is this point in?" A small change to the input moves the point a little. Whether the answer changes depends on what the map looks like around that point.

Figure 1 shows the intuition, but "compact, well separated, smooth border" is a picture, not a definition. A real solution would turn it into measurable properties and show which ones actually track robustness. The candidates I find most promising:

PropertyThe question it asks
MarginHow far is a typical point from the nearest decision border, measured inside the representation?
SmoothnessIf the input moves a little, does the representation also move only a little — or can a tiny change send it flying?
Cluster shapeAre classes tight and round, or long and thin with arms reaching towards other classes?
DimensionalityDoes the data use only a few directions in the space, or does it spread into many directions an attacker could exploit?
TopologyIs each class one connected piece, or broken into islands with gaps between them where errors can hide? (see OP-04, OP-05)

The key idea: robustness as a property of the map

One observation makes me think this is a real, findable property and not just a vague hope. Models trained to be robust — usually by practizing on attacked examples during training — turn out to learn different representations, not just a different final layer. Their internal features tend to line up better with what humans find meaningful, and are less dependent on tiny textures and pixel patterns that people cannot see.

There is also strong evidence that ordinary models rely on such invisible patterns because they are genuinely useful for getting the training labels right. In other words, a standard network is not "broken" — it is using real but fragile clues. A robust representation is one built from clues that do not vanish under small changes.

If that is right, robustness is something the representation has, not only something the model does under attack. And if it is something the representation has, we should be able to measure it there.

Why is it hard?

  • High dimensions are unintuitive. Representations often have hundreds or thousands of numbers. Distances, volumes and "nearness" behave very differently there than in the two-dimensional picture in Figure 1.
  • Correlation is not explanation. Robust models differ from standard ones in many ways at once. Finding a property that merely goes along with robustness is easy; showing that it causes robustness is hard.
  • Robust against what? A model can be robust to one kind of change and fragile to another. A single property may only explain one kind of robustness (see OP-03).
  • Every layer is different. The geometry of early, middle and late layers can tell different stories. It is not obvious which layer — or which combination — carries the explanation.
  • Accuracy may pull the other way. There is evidence that robustness and clean accuracy can be in tension, so a "perfectly robust" representation may give up some accuracy.

What has been tried so far

Several lines of work each capture part of the picture:

  • Adversarial training — training on attacked inputs so the model learns to resist them. Still the most reliable way to get robustness in practice, but it explains little about why the result is robust, and it is tied to the attack used.
  • Large-margin training — pushing data points further from decision borders, sometimes inside hidden layers rather than only at the output. Close in spirit to Figure 1.
  • Controlling smoothness — limiting how much each layer can stretch its input (for example by constraining its weights), so small input changes cannot grow into large ones.
  • Certified robustness — methods such as randomized smoothing that come with a mathematical guarantee within a small radius. Strong guarantees, but only for a narrow kind of change.
  • Studying what robust models learn — comparing features of robust and standard models, and showing that standard models depend on real but fragile patterns in the data.

What is mostly missing is a side-by-side test: take many models with known robustness, compute many candidate properties of their representations, and ask which property explains the most — including the topological ones that have barely been tried.

How I would approach it

  1. Collect a family of models with the same architecture but very different robustness — standard training, adversarial training at several strengths, smoothness constraints, different data augmentation.
  2. For each model and each layer, compute the candidate properties from the table above: margin, smoothness, cluster shape, effective dimensionality and simple topological summaries.
  3. Ask which property best predicts measured robustness across the whole family — not just whether robust models score higher on average.
  4. Then test cause, not just correlation: train new models while directly encouraging the winning property (see OP-06) and check whether robustness follows, without ever showing the model an attack.

Questions still open

  1. Is there one property of the representation that explains robustness across many kinds of attack and corruption, or one per kind?
  2. Which layer matters most — the last one before the answer, or the middle of the network?
  3. Does topology (how many pieces each class forms, whether there are holes) add anything beyond margin and smoothness? (see OP-04, OP-05)
  4. If we encourage that property directly during training, do we get robustness "for free", or does it only appear alongside adversarial training?
  5. Is the tension between robustness and accuracy a fact about the data, or a side effect of how we currently train?
  6. Do quantum models, whose internal states live in a very different kind of space, follow the same rules? (see OP-07, OP-08)

This question also underlies its neighbors on the list: building robustness that carries over to unseen attacks (OP-03), and warning that a model is about to fail (OP-01) — both become easier if we know what a healthy representation looks like.
