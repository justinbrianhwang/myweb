Engineering Paper Writing Method Guide

A section-by-section field guide to writing engineering research papers — not about generating ideas, but about the act of writing the paper itself. However strong your method is, a paper that is written badly does not get accepted. Acceptance is a writing problem as much as a research problem.

The guide follows the real order of work: understand the structure, outline in bullets, iterate on the outline, and only then draft each section on purpose. Every section ends in something you can copy — a worked example, an annotated paragraph or a fill-in template.

01 The Big Picture

What a paper actually is, where it begins, and how the pieces fit together before you write a single sentence.

A paper is a shared record of research

A paper is the process of sharing your research so other people can see it, trust it, and build on it. Stripped down, it is an experimental argument: you take an existing technique and the technique you built, compare them under fair conditions, and openly show which is better and why. That act of comparing and disclosing is the paper.

Before any of that, though, comes the hardest skill: finding a problem worth solving. Being able to locate a real, tractable problem in the first place is what distinguishes a mature researcher — the entire arc of a master's or PhD is repeated practice at finding and solving problems. This guide assumes you have a problem and a method, and focuses on the step people neglect: turning that work into an accepted paper.

The scope of this guide is deliberately narrow but decisive: how to write the paper. No matter how good the method is, if you cannot write it well, it will not be accepted — and an unaccepted result may as well not exist to the community. (Note: this guide does not cover writing survey papers, which are a different genre with their own conventions.)

Don't start by writing — start by outlining

Never write a paper by opening a blank file and typing full prose from the top. First outline: lay out, in bullets, why each piece belongs and how the argument flows. Fix the structure — sections, subsections, the order of claims — while it is still cheap to move things around. Only once the skeleton holds up do you expand bullets into paragraphs.

A powerful way to learn this is to work in reverse: take a finished, published paper and, sentence by sentence, write down the phrase or bullet that each sentence came from. Reconstructing the skeleton of a good paper teaches you the shape you are aiming for — and it is a genuinely useful exercise in its own right.

The anatomy of a paper

The content you truly author lives in Sections 1–5 (Introduction, Related Work, Method, Experiments, Conclusion). The reference list, by contrast, is generated automatically in LaTeX — so learn that tooling once and spend your real effort on the sections. If you can carry an argument through the standard flow below, your draft already has the shape of a paper:

  1. A problem that needs solvingWhy the reader should care at all.
  2. Prior techniques that tried to address itGrouped into a few families, not listed one by one.
  3. The limitation those techniques shareOne clear gap — the sentence the whole paper hangs on.
  4. Therefore, we propose a new techniqueThe idea in one or two sentences.
  5. …and we show, experimentally, that it is betterNumbers, under fair conditions, against the prior techniques.

The running example on this page is CHITIN: A Comprehensive In-thread Instruction Replication Technique Against Transient Faults, a software fault-tolerance paper built on the SRMT idea. It follows exactly this spine: hardware is vulnerable to transient faults → prior hardware/software approaches exist → they share limitations (cost, coverage) → so we propose a software instruction-replication technique → and we demonstrate its coverage and overhead.

Two summaries, nested. The Introduction is a summary of the whole paper, and the Abstract is a summary of the Introduction. Everything except the Abstract and Introduction maps roughly one-to-one onto the work you actually did — which is why those two are the hardest and the most read.

Writing is not the end — the review loop is

Submitting is a milestone, not the finish line. Understand the difference between a conference and a journal (Section 13), because it changes deadlines, expectations, and what "done" means. When reviews come back, you revise and write a response letter that explains, point by point, how you addressed each comment. For a conference, acceptance also means you present the work. Writing well up front is what makes this loop short instead of brutal.

The order you write in is not the order you read in

Readers go Title → Abstract → Introduction → figures → Conclusion, and only some of them read the middle. Writers should work almost in reverse, starting from what is already settled and ending with the parts that summarise everything else:

  1. Figures and tables of the main resultsThey fix what the paper can actually claim. If a claim has no figure or table behind it, it probably does not belong in the paper.
  2. MethodYou built it, so you can describe it. Writing it first also exposes gaps in your own understanding.
  3. ExperimentsSetup first, then one paragraph per figure or table.
  4. Related WorkEasier once you know exactly how your method differs.
  5. IntroductionNow you know the real contribution — not the one you planned months ago.
  6. Conclusion, Abstract and TitlePure summaries of what is already written. Doing them last keeps them true.
Time budget. A common rule of thumb: plan for writing to take at least as long as you expect, then add a full week for revision. Aim to have a complete draft — every section present, even if rough — about two weeks before the deadline, so your advisor and co-authors have time for at least two rounds of comments.

A page budget before the first sentence

Venues give a hard page limit. Decide in advance roughly how many pages each section gets, and write to that budget; it is far easier than cutting a full page from finished text the night before the deadline. A typical split for an 8-page engineering conference paper (adjust to your venue and field):

SectionApprox. lengthNotes
Abstract150–250 wordsCheck the venue's word limit.
Introduction~1–1.25 pagesOften includes a teaser figure.
Background / Related Work~0.75–1 pageShorter if folded into the Introduction.
Method~2–2.5 pagesOverview figure plus subsections.
Experiments~2–2.5 pagesMost of the figures and tables live here.
Conclusion~0.25 pageOne or two paragraphs.

02 Outlining → Drafting

The single most leveraged habit in paper writing. Master this and every section gets easier.

“Outlining is the phase when you write in bullets. You do not need complete sentences — just phrases. Try to fix the structure of the paper: write down the sections and subsections, but inside them there are no sentences, just bullets. Your arguments, and the flow of the arguments, are the main thing you are trying to fix. Once you have a version of the outline, you iterate on it and refine it until the story is set. It is much easier to iterate on an outline than on writing, because (i) the outline is smaller, so you can see and think about all of it at once, and (ii) fixing it is much easier than fixing writing.”
— MPS Lab, “How to Write a Paper”

The expansion ladder

Write each section as a single main bullet (its job in one line). Break that into sub-bullets that carry the detail, the sub-claims, and the evidence. Then expand each sub-bullet into prose. By the time you reach prose, the hard thinking — what to say, in what order, and why — is already done, so drafting becomes almost mechanical.

Worked example — one Introduction paragraph, three stages
1 · Main bullet
  • Prior software fault-tolerance is too slow to use.
2 · Sub-bullets
  • transient faults: growing with smaller transistors
  • software replication: no new hardware needed
  • but: 2–3× slowdown, partial coverage
  • → need low overhead and full coverage
3 · Paragraph

As transistors shrink, transient faults become a practical threat to correct execution. Software-only replication is attractive because it needs no new hardware, but existing schemes slow programs down by two to three times and still leave some instructions unprotected. A practical technique must therefore keep overhead low without giving up coverage.

Illustrative sentences written for this guide, not quoted from the paper. Notice that every sentence in the paragraph maps back to exactly one sub-bullet.

The reason this works is economic. Fixing a ten-line outline is trivial; restructuring eight pages of finished prose is painful and error-prone. Every structural problem you catch at the outline stage is a problem you never have to untangle later.

Lead with the point (top-down). State the key claim first, then add support. Reviewers stay oriented, and each paragraph announces its purpose before defending it. Professors who write this way — stating the important concept up front, then elaborating — are consistently easier to read.
When a paper breaks the standard structure, it is usually on purpose. Page limits, a nonstandard contribution, or a venue's house style can all justify deviation. Early on, follow the canonical structure; later, learn to read why an author departed from it — that reading skill is part of becoming a writer.

What a whole-paper outline looks like

Before drafting, the entire paper should fit on one or two pages of bullets. Each section has its one-line job (the main bullet), and under it the claims in the order they will appear. This is the document you show your advisor first — it takes five minutes to read and catches structural problems that would take days to fix in prose.

Worked example — a whole-paper outline (illustrative)
  • 1 Introduction — transient faults are a growing threat; software protection is too slow; we make it practical.
    • faults ↑ as transistors shrink → silent data corruption
    • hardware redundancy: effective but costly
    • software replication: no new hardware, but 2–3× slowdown or partial coverage
    • our idea: replicate in-thread, check only where errors escape
    • contributions (3) + headline result
  • 2 Background & Related Work — fault model; prior hardware and software schemes and their limits.
    • fault model: single bit flip in registers / functional units
    • hardware: lockstep, redundant multithreading
    • software: instruction duplication, SRMT → overhead / coverage gaps
  • 3 Method — how in-thread replication and check placement work.
    • 3.1 overview (Fig. 3)
    • 3.2 instruction replication
    • 3.3 check placement
    • 3.4 special cases: calls, memory, I/O
  • 4 Evaluation — is it correct, how much does it cost, which parts matter?
    • 4.1 setup
    • 4.2 fault coverage (fault injection)
    • 4.3 runtime overhead vs. baselines
    • 4.4 ablation of check placement
  • 5 Conclusion — summary, limits (single-threaded only), future work.

Notice that every main bullet is a full claim, not a topic word. “Method” tells you nothing; “how in-thread replication and check placement work” tells you what the section must deliver.

How to iterate on the outline

  1. Read only the main bullets, top to bottom. They should tell the whole story by themselves, like the spine on the previous diagram. If one does not follow from the one before, the structure is wrong — fix it now.
  2. Check that every claim has evidence. Next to each sub-bullet that makes a claim, note where the support comes from: a citation, a figure, a table or a proof. Unsupported claims either get evidence or get cut.
  3. Check that every result has a promise. Each experiment in Section 4 should answer a question raised earlier. An experiment that answers nothing in the Introduction is either a missing contribution or a distraction.
  4. Sketch the figures. Mark where each figure goes and write its caption as a bullet. Figures decide how much text you need around them.
  5. Get feedback on the outline, not the draft. Ask your advisor and co-authors to comment at this stage, when a change costs one line instead of one page.

From sub-bullet to paragraph

When you finally write prose, each paragraph should do one job. A reliable shape:

  1. Topic sentenceThe point of the paragraph, stated first. A reader skimming only first sentences should still follow the paper.
  2. SupportExplanation, evidence, citation or example — the sub-bullets, now as sentences.
  3. LinkOptional last sentence that hands over to the next paragraph (“This motivates…”, “However, …”).
The first-sentence test. Copy only the first sentence of every paragraph into a new file. If that file reads like a coherent summary of the paper, your structure is working. If it reads like a list of unrelated facts, the topic sentences are missing or buried.

03 Title & Abstract

The two things every reviewer, every search engine and every future reader see first.

Writing the title

A good title tells the reader what problem and what kind of solution, in about 8–15 words. It is a search query as much as a headline: people find papers by the words in the title.

  • Name the problem and the approach. “Low-Overhead Software Fault Detection via In-Thread Instruction Replication” says both.
  • Use the terms people search for. Prefer the field's standard vocabulary over a clever phrase nobody types into a search box.
  • A system name is fine — NAME: description is a common, useful pattern — but the part after the colon must still stand on its own.
  • Avoid empty words: “A Novel…”, “Towards…” (unless the work really is preliminary), “An Efficient…” — every paper claims to be novel and efficient.
Vague

“A Novel Approach for Improving Reliability in Computer Systems”

Specific

“Low-Overhead Detection of Transient Faults through In-Thread Instruction Replication”

Writing the Abstract

The Abstract is a summary of the Introduction, and it is often the only part a reviewer reads before deciding how carefully to read the rest. Write it last, as one paragraph, one sentence (or two) per move:

Template — the five-move Abstract

1 · Context. Problem area is important because why it matters, ideally with a concrete consequence.

2 · Gap. However, existing family of methods shared limitation.

3 · Idea. In this paper, we propose name, which key idea in one clause.

4 · Evidence. On benchmarks / datasets, name headline result with a number compared with strongest baseline.

5 · Takeaway. These results show that what the reader should remember.

Annotated example — a complete Abstract

Transient hardware faults are becoming more frequent as transistors shrink, and they can silently corrupt program output. Software-only fault detection avoids hardware changes, but existing schemes either slow programs down substantially or leave part of the code unprotected. We present an in-thread instruction replication technique that duplicates computation within the same thread and places comparisons only where corrupted values could leave the program. In fault-injection experiments on standard benchmarks, our technique detects nearly all injected faults while incurring lower runtime overhead than prior software approaches. These results show that full-coverage software fault detection can be practical on commodity processors.

Illustrative Abstract written for this guide. In a real Abstract, the Evidence sentence should carry an actual number (e.g. coverage and overhead percentages).

Abstract checklist

  • Within the venue's word limit; one paragraph; no citations, no undefined abbreviations.
  • Contains at least one concrete number.
  • Every claim in it is supported somewhere in the paper — reviewers check.
  • Uses the same name for your method as the rest of the paper.
  • Readable by someone outside your sub-field.

04 Introduction

The most important — and the hardest — section to write. Budget the most iterations here.

Every paper's Introduction looks broadly similar (the later sections vary far more from paper to paper), because its job is always the same: convince the reviewers of how important the problem is and how significant your contribution is. Reviewers judge from the Abstract and Introduction first; if there is nothing compelling there, many will not read on. Today some readers even paste papers into an LLM for a summary — but that summary is still built from your Intro. First impressions decide, so a good Introduction is written for a broad audience: an undergraduate, not only a specialist, should be able to follow it.

The opening: give a non-expert the background

Provide the background a reader without deep domain expertise needs. A reliable recipe for the opening blends three ingredients:

  • (A) General background — the broad context and motivation of the area.
  • (C) Field-specific terms — define the vocabulary the paper depends on. Calibrate to the era and audience: a term that needed explaining years ago may now be assumed. In 1996 you had to define "data mining"; today you simply cite prior work and move on. Explaining a now-common keyword wastes space.
  • (B) The problem with existing / general approaches — what is missing, broken, or insufficient about how the field currently handles this.

Explaining the general research setting is itself part of the background. The goal is that a reader arrives at your contribution already understanding why it is needed.

From existing methods to your contribution

If existing solutions had no problem, there would be no reason to propose a new one — so the Introduction has to establish that a gap exists. The move is: briefly mention and summarize prior techniques (briefly — this is the Intro, not Related Work), classify them, then critique their shared limitation. The rhetorical pattern is:

  1. Existing methods, summarizedTwo or three families, one sentence each.
  2. Their common limitationStated precisely and fairly — this is where reviewers push back.
  3. We solve that common limitationThe key idea, not the mechanism.
  4. …and performance improvesOne headline number that proves it.

An equally good framing is the "gap" version: "this capability simply did not exist before, so we built it." Both end in the same place — a clearly stated contribution that the reviewer can see is new.

Skeleton trick: take a strong Introduction and bold only the sentences that name a limitation of prior work. What remains in bold is the argument's skeleton — a clean chain of "this was missing → we address it." Write your own Intro so that this skeleton would be equally visible.
Anticipate the reviewer. The limitation and contribution sentences are where reviewers push back hardest. Writing them precisely and fairly — not overclaiming, not attacking — is exactly what reduces pushback later. A sentence that overstates your novelty is an invitation for a reviewer to find a counterexample.

An Introduction, annotated

Below, each rhetorical move of a compact Introduction is tinted and tagged. Read only the tags from top to bottom and you have the outline back.

Annotated example — what each sentence is doing

As transistors shrink and supply voltages drop, processors become increasingly vulnerable to transient faults — brief bit flips caused by particle strikes or electrical noise. Such faults rarely crash a program; instead they silently corrupt its output, a failure known as silent data corruption (SDC).

Hardware redundancy can detect these faults, but it is costly to add to commodity processors. Software-based replication avoids new hardware, yet existing schemes either duplicate every instruction, slowing programs by more than 2×, or protect only part of the code, leaving faults undetected.

In this paper, we propose an in-thread replication technique that duplicates instructions within the same thread and checks them only at points where corrupted values would leave the program. Our contributions are (1) a replication scheme that covers all instructions, (2) a compiler pass that places checks to minimise overhead, and (3) an evaluation on standard benchmarks. Across these benchmarks, our technique detects nearly all injected faults while reducing runtime overhead relative to prior software schemes.

Illustrative text written for this guide in the spirit of the running example; it is not quoted from the paper, and the numbers are left qualitative on purpose.

What the Introduction should contain

  1. Background and field terms, pitched for a general reader (A + C).
  2. Existing approaches and the limitation they share (B).
  3. A short, high-level statement of your proposed method — what it is, in one or two sentences (not the mechanism, just the idea).
  4. Your contributions and their effect (e.g., "our decoding scheme hides the loss of an entire frame").
  5. A brief mention of results / conclusions to signal that the claims are backed up.
  6. (Optional) a paper-structure paragraph ("Section 2 reviews… Section 3 presents…") — include only if space allows; drop it first when you are over the page budget.

Build this skeleton first, then flesh it out during outlining: each of items 1–6 becomes a sub-bullet, then a paragraph. Expect the Introduction to take the most drafts of any section — it genuinely is the hardest, and that is normal.

Writing the contributions list

Most engineering papers end the Introduction with a short, numbered list of contributions. Each item should be verifiable: a reviewer must be able to point to the section that delivers it.

Vague

“We propose a novel and efficient framework that significantly improves reliability.”

Checkable

“We propose a compiler pass that places fault checks only at store and branch instructions (Section 4), and show that it lowers overhead on all benchmarks (Section 5.2).”

Paragraph by paragraph

Most engineering Introductions have four to six paragraphs. Here is what each one does and how to start it:

¶JobTypical opening
1Why the area matters — broad context and stakes“X is widely used in…”, “As … grows, …”
2The specific problem, with the terms it needs“A key challenge in X is…”
3What has been tried, grouped, and the shared limitation“Prior work addresses this by… However, …”
4Your key idea and why it avoids that limitation“In this paper, we propose… Our key insight is that…”
5Contributions (often a numbered list) and headline results“This paper makes the following contributions:”
6(optional) Paper organisation“The rest of this paper is organized as follows…”

State your key insight explicitly

Reviewers look for the one idea that makes your method work where others did not. Do not make them reconstruct it — write a sentence that begins “Our key insight is that…” or “The main idea is…”. If you cannot complete that sentence, the contribution is not clear yet, and no amount of polishing later sections will fix that.

No insight

“We propose a new method that combines several techniques to improve performance.”

Insight stated

“Our key insight is that a corrupted value only matters once it leaves the processor — through a store, a branch or a system call — so checks are needed only at those points.”

The teaser figure

Many strong papers put a small figure on page 1 or 2 that shows the problem or the idea at a glance — for example, the prior approach and yours side by side, or a plot where your method sits in the best corner of a trade-off (lower overhead, higher coverage). A reviewer who understands the teaser is already on your side before reaching Section 3.

Common Introduction mistakes

  • Starting too broad. “Artificial intelligence has changed the world” spends a sentence saying nothing. Start one step above your actual problem, not five.
  • A history lesson. Listing prior papers one by one belongs in Related Work. Here, group them and name their shared limitation.
  • Hiding the contribution. If your idea first appears on page 2, many reviewers will already have formed an opinion without it.
  • Overclaiming. “Solves”, “eliminates”, “guarantees”, “for the first time” — each is a challenge to the reviewer to find the exception. Claim exactly what your experiments show.
  • Contributions that cannot be checked. Every item should point to a section, a figure or a table.

05 Related Work & Citations

Where you prove you know the field — and place your work within it. A weak survey here can sink an otherwise good paper.

What Related Work does

Related Work surveys prior research on the same problem, organizes it, and states its limitations. Think of it as expanding into full paragraphs what the Introduction covered in a sentence or two. It shows the reviewer that you genuinely know the literature, and by making prior limitations explicit it makes it obvious why your work was necessary — which indirectly strengthens the case for your contribution. It is also where you can supply the background a reader needs to follow the rest of the paper.

Tone matters: do not attack prior work too aggressively. Where useful, explain the context your paper grew out of, and acknowledge what earlier methods got right before turning to what they missed.

A poor survey can be an outright reject reason. Reviewers use Related Work to test whether you actually command the field. If key prior work is missing, expect comments like "the authors appear unaware of [X]" — and that alone can end a submission. A thorough, fair survey is table stakes for credibility.

Practical structure

  • Open with a table classifying prior methods into categories, then give each category a subsection.
  • Within a subsection, describe the approach briefly, then pivot to its limitation — often with a "However, …". You must describe an approach at least roughly before you are entitled to criticize it.
  • Lead with the key concept, then elaborate (top-down), the same discipline as everywhere else.
  • Write your own research category in more depth than the others — that is the neighborhood your contribution lives in, so readers need it in sharper focus.
  • A figure comparing an existing method against your method can cut length while improving clarity. Under a strict page budget (e.g., a 6-page conference paper) this is often necessary — and adding a figure is usually easier than rewriting paragraphs.
  • If you deliberately omit a line of work, you can say so and why.
  • When your paper builds directly on one concept (in the example, SRMT), give that concept more room — sometimes even its own section — so its variants and your relationship to them are unmistakable. Skipping this invites "how does this differ from SRMT variant Y?" pushback.

One Related Work paragraph, annotated

Annotated example — describe, credit, then contrast

Software-based redundancy. SRMT [3] runs a redundant copy of the program in a second thread and compares the two threads' results to detect faults. This removes the need for dedicated hardware and works on unmodified processors. However, communication between the two threads adds substantial overhead, and the scheme relies on a spare core being available. Our technique keeps the redundant instructions in the same thread, so it needs neither inter-thread communication nor an extra core.

Illustrative paragraph. The pattern is the point: describe the work fairly, credit what it does well, state its limitation with “However,” and end on how your work differs.

When there is no Related Work section

Sometimes Related Work is folded into the Introduction, leaving only a short Preliminaries paragraph — usually to satisfy a page limit (the DATE conference, for instance, caps papers at 6 pages; a comparison table may even be pushed into the Intro). Some venues instead move Related Work to the end of the paper so the method comes first. Each choice trades off reader guidance against momentum. When you are new, follow the standard placement; deviate only with a clear reason.

References & citation: why they matter

A reference is an idea you took from prior work — so cite as much as you legitimately can. Citing meaningful papers gives proper credit, and citation counts are a rough proxy for impact: a landmark paper may accumulate hundreds of thousands of citations (some exceed 200k), and that reach is what "high impact" means. Journals summarize this as an impact factor derived from references; it is an imperfect, sometimes controversial metric. Conferences are judged differently — and in computer science, top conferences are frequently treated at journal level.

Field note (CS): in computer science, a strong conference paper is real career credit and can be rated above a journal. Community-curated venue tiers (e.g., the Pusnow ranking list) are a common reference for where a venue sits. In LaTeX, citation formatting is automatic; Korean-language venues written in HWP require formatting each entry by hand, per the target style.

Paraphrase — never copy-paste

You may quote directly on occasion, but the default is to restate prior work in your own words and cite it. Never reproduce sentences verbatim — summarize or rephrase — but keep technical terms unchanged (don't "creatively" rename an established concept). Careless referencing invites plagiarism concerns; this matters doubly for surveys, which are citation-dense and must be paraphrased throughout.

Copying (don't)

"SRMT duplicates instructions within a single thread and compares the results to detect transient faults." — lifted word-for-word from the source.

Paraphrase + cite (do)

"Prior in-thread replication schemes such as SRMT re-execute instructions inside one thread and cross-check the outputs, catching transient faults without extra hardware [3]."

Finding and organizing the literature

  • Citation chaining — walk a paper's references backward and its citing papers forward. Especially valuable at the start of a project for mapping a field quickly.
  • Google Scholar shows which papers cite a given work — a fast way to move forward in time and to find the papers whose claims support yours (paraphrase those to strengthen your own argument).
  • Survey papers are field maps and excellent guideposts when entering a new area. They are authoritative but long (70–80 pages is normal) and heavily cited — read them for orientation, and never copy from them.
  • Collect candidate papers into a running list first, then organize and classify. Some papers even publish their literature search strategy; studying those teaches you how to search well yourself.
  • Watch citation position so markers don't blur together — e.g., when [2] and [3] would otherwise be ambiguous, place each next to the claim it supports.

Building the section, step by step

  1. CollectGather every paper you might cite into one list (a reference manager or a plain .bib file). Note one line per paper: what it does and its limitation relative to yours.
  2. GroupSort the list into two to four families by approach — not by year and not by author. Each family becomes a paragraph or subsection with a short title.
  3. OrderPut the families furthest from your work first and the closest last, so the section ends next to your contribution.
  4. Write each familyDescribe the shared idea, cite the main papers, credit what they achieve, then state the limitation that matters for your problem.
  5. Close with the differenceEnd each family, or the whole section, with one sentence that says how your work differs. Never leave the reader to infer it.

Citing well at the sentence level

  • A citation is not a noun. Write “Smith et al. [4] proposed…” or “…as shown in prior work [4]”, not “[4] proposed…”.
  • Group citations for a shared claim: “Several schemes duplicate instructions in software [3, 7, 9].”
  • Cite the original, not a later paper that happened to repeat the idea.
  • Be fair in tone. “X does not address Y” is a fact; “X fails miserably at Y” is an attack that a reviewer — who may be X's author — will remember.
  • Include recent work. A Related Work section with nothing from the last two or three years suggests you have not checked the current state of the field.

06 Method

Comparatively the easiest section to write — if you can clearly explain what you built.

The Method section is the record of "found a problem → built a solution." If you can explain your technique clearly, you can write this section. Depending on the technique, describe how it was designed and how it operates, in enough detail that a competent reader could reconstruct it.

Structure

  • Give an overview first — a single diagram or paragraph showing how the whole system works — then break the details into subsections, ideally one figure per subsection.
  • Cover three things overall: how you solve the problem, what differs from prior methods, and by how much you improve on them.
  • The sentence immediately before a subsection should announce what that subsection will do (top-down). Keeping a consistent order across subsections makes the reader's job dramatically easier.
  • When a point is central, it is fine to spend more space on it — and sometimes to pull "why this matters" into its own short section.

How a Method section reads

  1. Overview paragraph + overview figure“Figure 3 shows the overall flow of our technique. It has three stages: …” — the reader gets the whole system before any detail.
  2. One subsection per stageSame order as the figure. Each opens with one sentence saying what the stage does and why it is needed.
  3. Details inside each subsectionAlgorithm, equations or a small example, then the design choice and why it beats the obvious alternative.
  4. Discussion of special casesWhat happens at the edges (e.g. library calls, memory errors) — the questions a reviewer would otherwise ask.
Template — the first sentence of each subsection

“To goal of this stage, we what the stage does. Unlike prior approach, which its drawback, this advantage.”

Figure-first. If you're stuck on the outline, draw the figure first. Once you have a clear overview diagram of how the system works, the explanation — and then the text — follows naturally. A single well-drawn overview figure can be reused throughout the paper. A common and effective device: draw the flaw in the prior approach beside how your system fixes it, then write the paragraph that simply explains the figure.
Study the neighbors first. Before writing, read a few papers close to yours and observe how they are organized — section order, what goes in a figure vs. text, how much math. Prefer published papers over arXiv preprints, since a preprint may not have passed review and may model habits you don't want to copy. In the end, the only job of this section is to explain your technique effectively.

How much detail is enough?

The standard is reproducibility by a competent reader: someone in your field should be able to re-implement the method from the paper alone. In practice, that means every design decision is stated, every parameter has a value (here or in the setup), and every non-obvious choice has a reason. Implementation trivia — file formats, variable names, which library function you called — usually does not belong unless it affects the result.

Notation and equations

  • Define every symbol the first time it appears, right next to the equation (“where N is the number of instructions and…”).
  • Keep notation consistent across the whole paper. If x is an input in Section 3, it cannot be a weight in Section 4. A notation table helps in math-heavy papers.
  • Equations are part of sentences and take punctuation. Number only the ones you refer to later.
  • Explain the intuition in words before or after a formula. Many reviewers skim equations and read the sentence around them.

Algorithms and pseudocode

Use pseudocode when the order of steps matters and prose would be ambiguous. Keep it short (roughly 10–25 lines), number the lines, and refer to them in the text (“lines 4–7 duplicate each arithmetic instruction”). Pseudocode does not replace explanation — it supports it.

Walk through a small example

After describing a mechanism in general terms, show it on a tiny concrete case: three instructions, a small graph, a 4×4 matrix. One worked example, ideally paired with a figure, often explains more than a page of general description — and it is the part reviewers remember.

Annotated example — opening a Method subsection

To detect a fault before it can corrupt program output, our technique must compare original and duplicated values at the right points. We therefore insert a comparison immediately before every instruction through which a value can leave the processor: stores, conditional branches and system calls (Algorithm 1, lines 5–9). Unlike schemes that compare after every duplicated instruction, this placement lets most duplicated instructions run without a check, which is the main source of our overhead reduction (Section 4.3).

Illustrative text written for this guide, not quoted from a published paper.

07 Experiments

Setup, results, analysis, contribution — and, above everything, reproducibility.

The Experiments section answers four questions: How was the experiment set up? What results came out? How were they analyzed? How much did the work contribute? Describe the environment and procedure precisely enough that someone else could rebuild it, and explain the performance metrics that are standard in your field (accuracy, latency, coverage, overhead — whatever your problem is measured by).

More experiments is (almost always) better. Thin evaluation reliably draws an "insufficient experiments" review. Some venues — DAC is a classic example — accept more readily when the evaluation is extensive, provided you have the compute to run it. Additional and controlled experiments back your claims and leave reviewers little to grab onto.

Reproducibility

  • Report the full experimental environment; where possible, open-source your code and results, and disclose the models used, the number of epochs, and how training was done. Many papers now release everything by default.
  • If a baseline's code is not public, you may have to re-implement it from the paper — and then match the environment as closely as possible. Hardware differences are real: if the original ran on an RTX 3060 and you run on an RTX 5090, raw timings are not comparable, so you must control for that (same hardware, or normalized reporting).
  • The reason for all of this is that results others cannot reproduce are results reviewers cannot trust.

Presenting and defending results

  • Present results mostly as graphs and tables, emphasizing your result clearly.
  • Run an ablation study — systematically remove one component at a time to show what each contributes. (Memorize this term; reviewers expect it.) It is how you prove that each part of your method earns its place.
  • Defend the cases where you do well and where you do not. Honestly addressing weak spots pre-empts the reviewer who would otherwise raise them.
  • For a genuinely new problem there may be no existing baseline; then explain more carefully, or construct a reasonable baseline yourself to compare against.
  • Add experiments that back the theory from multiple angles. If a difference looks small from one view, show it from another — e.g., two trajectories that look identical in 2D can differ dramatically when plotted in 3D.

Structure of the section

  1. Experimental setupHardware, software versions, benchmarks or datasets, baselines, metrics, and how many runs.
  2. Main resultOne figure or table that answers the paper’s central question against all baselines.
  3. AblationRemove one component at a time — each row shows what that component contributes.
  4. Sensitivity and costBehaviour as parameters change; overheads such as time, memory and code size.
  5. Discussion and limitsWhere the method does worse, and why.

Writing a results paragraph

Never leave a figure to speak for itself. Every results paragraph makes three moves: what the figure shows, the number that matters, and why it happens.

Annotated example

Figure 6 compares the runtime overhead of our technique with two software baselines across all benchmarks. Our technique has the lowest overhead on every benchmark, with the largest gap on memory-intensive programs. This is because checks are placed only before stores and branches, so most duplicated instructions run without an immediate comparison. The gap narrows on compute-bound programs, where nearly every instruction feeds a later store.

Illustrative paragraph; in a real paper, the second sentence carries the actual numbers.

Avoid default charts. Effort is visible, and so is its absence. Make series distinguishable even in black & white (papers are often printed grayscale): use distinct markers, line styles, and hatching — not just a red line vs. a blue line, which can be indistinguishable to colorblind readers and in grayscale. A clear, deliberate figure reads as a careful paper.

Start from research questions

A clean way to organise an evaluation is to state a few explicit questions at the start and answer one per subsection:

Example research questions (illustrative)
  • RQ1 — Effectiveness: How many injected faults does the technique detect?
  • RQ2 — Cost: What runtime and code-size overhead does it add, compared with the baselines?
  • RQ3 — Design choices: How much does each component (here: check placement) contribute?
  • RQ4 — Sensitivity: How do the results change with workload type and input size?

What the setup subsection must contain

  • Hardware: processor or GPU model, memory, number of nodes.
  • Software: OS, compiler or framework versions, important flags.
  • Workloads: benchmarks or datasets, with versions, sizes and splits; why these were chosen.
  • Baselines: which methods, which implementation (official code or your re-implementation), and the settings used.
  • Metrics: definitions for anything non-standard, and which direction is better.
  • Protocol: number of runs or seeds, how results are aggregated (mean, median), and how variance is reported.
  • Hyperparameters: values, and how they were tuned — and that baselines received comparable tuning.

Fair baselines

  • Compare against the strongest relevant methods, not the easiest to beat. A reviewer will name the one you left out.
  • Give baselines the same budget: same hardware, same data, similar tuning effort. Tuning your method for weeks and running baselines with defaults is a common reason for rejection.
  • Where you re-implemented a baseline, say so, and show that your version reproduces the original paper's reported numbers where possible.

Reporting numbers

  • Report variance (standard deviation, confidence intervals or min/max over runs) whenever results come from randomness or noisy measurements.
  • Be precise about relative vs. absolute changes: from 20% to 15% overhead is a 5 percentage-point drop, or a 25% relative reduction. Say which one you mean.
  • Use a sensible number of digits. “73.4%” is information; “73.41827%” is noise.
  • Highlight the best result in each table column (commonly in bold), and state that convention in the caption.
  • Never report only the benchmarks where you win. If a subset is shown, explain the choice and give the full results in an appendix or supplement.

08 Figures & Tables

Most reviewers look at the figures before they read the text. Design them to carry the argument alone.

Which kind of figure?

You want to showUse
How the system worksAn overview diagram: boxes and arrows, left-to-right or top-to-bottom, with as few labels as possible
Your method vs. baselines across benchmarksA grouped bar chart, or a table if exact numbers matter
A trend as a parameter changesA line plot with markers
A trade-off (e.g. overhead vs. coverage)A scatter plot, with the “better” corner indicated
Exact values many readers will compareA table
Qualitative behaviour (images, trajectories)A grid of examples, including failure cases

Making figures readable

  • Font size in the figure should match the caption at print size. Axis labels that are unreadable on paper are among the most common review comments.
  • Label axes with units (“Runtime overhead (%)”), and say which direction is better if it is not obvious.
  • Distinguish series by more than colour: markers, line styles, hatching. Check the figure in grayscale.
  • Use the same colour for the same method in every figure of the paper.
  • Export as vector (PDF) from your plotting tool so text and lines stay sharp.
  • Remove chart junk: 3-D effects, heavy gridlines, shadows and redundant legends add nothing.

Writing captions

A caption should let a reader understand the figure without searching the text: what is shown, how to read it, and — ideally — the takeaway.

Unhelpful

“Figure 5: Results.”

Self-contained

“Figure 5: Runtime overhead normalised to the unprotected program (lower is better). Our technique has the lowest overhead on all benchmarks; the gap is largest on memory-intensive programs.”

Designing tables

  • Put the caption above a table (the usual convention in engineering venues) and below a figure.
  • Use horizontal rules only (booktabs in LaTeX); avoid vertical lines and grid boxes.
  • Align numbers on the decimal point, and use the same precision within a column.
  • Put your method in the last row (or first), consistently, and mark the best value per column.
  • If the table is wide, transpose it or split it — never shrink text below a readable size.
Every figure and table must be referenced in the text — and the text should say what to notice (“As Figure 5 shows, …”), not just “see Figure 5”. A figure the text never mentions looks like padding.

09 Conclusion

The most formulaic section — which is exactly why it is the easiest.

Conclusions are highly standardized in both structure and phrasing, so you can safely follow the usual template. Read a few conclusions in your target venue and you will see the same shape repeat. The core is three moves:

  1. An overall summary — restate the problem, the method, and the headline result in compact form.
  2. The limitations of your approach — stated plainly and without defensiveness.
  3. Future work — where this could go next; "it would be worth trying this in another domain" is a perfectly good, common form.
Annotated example — the three moves

This paper presented an in-thread instruction replication technique that detects transient faults in software, with full instruction coverage and lower overhead than prior software schemes. Our evaluation is limited to single-threaded programs, and faults in memory outside the processor are not covered. In future work, we plan to extend the technique to multi-threaded programs and to combine it with memory protection.

Illustrative paragraph.

Some venues require limitations explicitly. NeurIPS, for example, asks authors to state the limitations of their work. Cover summary, limitations, and future directions and reviewers very rarely push back on the Conclusion — it is the section least likely to cost you an acceptance, so don't overthink it.
Template — a three-move Conclusion

Summary. This paper presented name, a type of technique that key idea. On evaluation, it headline result.

Limitations. name currently assumption or scope it does not cover, and a second limitation, if any.

Future work. We plan to most natural extension and to second direction.

Common Conclusion mistakes

  • Copying the Abstract. Same content is fine; the same sentences are not. Rephrase, and you can be more specific now that the reader has seen the results.
  • New results or claims. Nothing should appear here for the first time.
  • Vague future work. “We will further improve the method” says nothing; name a concrete next step.

10 Sentence-Level Style

Small habits that make a technical paper easy to read — and that reviewers notice when they are missing.

Rules that cover most problems

  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs three commas and a semicolon, split it.
  • Prefer active voice when the actor matters: “We insert a check before each store” is clearer than “A check is inserted before each store.” Passive voice is fine when the actor is irrelevant.
  • “We” is standard in engineering papers for what the authors did, even with a single author in many venues — follow the venue's custom.
  • Tense: present for what the paper says and what is generally true (“Section 3 describes…”, “the method duplicates…”); past for what you did in experiments (“we ran each benchmark ten times”).
  • One term per concept. If you call it a “check” in Section 3, do not call it a “comparison”, a “validation” and a “verification” later. Readers assume different words mean different things.
  • Define abbreviations once at first use, and do not define ones you use only once or twice.
  • Cut empty intensifiers: “very”, “extremely”, “significantly” (unless you mean statistical significance), “clearly”, “obviously”.
  • Quantify instead of describing: “reduces overhead from 2.1× to 1.4×” beats “greatly reduces overhead”.
Wordy

“It is very important to note that the proposed approach is able to significantly reduce the overhead in a very efficient manner.”

Direct

“Our approach reduces runtime overhead from 2.1× to 1.4×.”

Inconsistent and passive

“In Section 3 the replication is described, and the checking is explained in Section 4, where the validation points are also discussed.”

Consistent and active

“Section 3 describes instruction replication; Section 4 describes where checks are placed.”

Claim exactly what you showed

Match the strength of each sentence to its evidence. “Our results suggest” for a trend seen on a few benchmarks; “our results show” for a consistent effect across all of them; “we prove” only with a proof. Words like always, never, optimal and first need either a proof or a very careful literature check.

Writing in English as a second language

  • Short sentences are not a weakness — they are what good technical English looks like.
  • Keep a personal list of phrases from well-written papers in your area (“To this end, …”, “In contrast to …”, “This allows us to …”) and reuse the structures, never whole sentences.
  • Run a grammar checker as a final pass, and if possible ask a fluent co-author to read the Abstract and Introduction.

11 Revising & Responding to Reviews

A first draft is raw material. Most of the quality comes from deliberate passes over it — and from how you answer reviewers.

Revise in passes, one concern at a time

Trying to fix structure, wording and typos in a single read means none of them gets fixed well. Do separate passes:

  1. Structure passRead only section titles and first sentences. Does the story hold? Is anything in the wrong place?
  2. Claims passFor every claim, find its evidence. Weaken, support or cut anything unsupported.
  3. Reader passGive the draft to someone who has not seen the project and ask where they got lost. Their confusion is a preview of the reviewer’s.
  4. Figures passLook at every figure and caption alone, at print size.
  5. Language passWording, consistency of terms, abbreviations, tense.
  6. Format passTemplate untouched, page limit, references complete, anonymisation if the venue is double-blind.
Leave time between passes. Even one night away from the draft makes problems visible that you could not see while writing. Reading the paper aloud — or printing it — catches a surprising number of errors.

Writing the response letter

When reviews come back with a revise decision (always for journals; in conferences with a rebuttal phase, in shortened form), you answer every comment. The format that works:

  1. Thank the reviewers briefly once at the top — no need to repeat it in every answer.
  2. Quote each comment, numbered (R1.1, R1.2, …), then answer it directly below.
  3. Say what you changed and where — section, page, and ideally quote the new text. Mark changes in the revised manuscript (coloured text or a diff).
  4. If you disagree, do it with evidence and politely: explain, show data, and where possible still add a clarification to the paper so the next reader does not have the same question.
  5. Never ignore a comment, even a small one. An unanswered point is read as a refusal.
Example — one response item (illustrative)

R2.3 — “The overhead comparison does not include [Baseline Z], which seems to be the closest prior work.”

Response: We thank the reviewer for pointing this out. We have added Baseline Z to the overhead comparison (Section 4.3, Figure 6) using the authors' public implementation with its default settings. Our technique remains lower in overhead on all benchmarks; the difference is smallest on compute-bound programs, which we now discuss in Section 4.3 (page 8, highlighted).

Rejection is normal. Most papers at competitive venues are rejected at least once. Read the reviews, wait a day, then treat them as free advice: fix what they found, and submit an improved version to the next suitable venue.

12 Using LLMs Responsibly

Genuinely useful help — under rules that vary by venue and change every single year.

LLMs can turn a set of bullets into draft paragraphs and help you brainstorm framings, and it is increasingly unrealistic to avoid them entirely. But they carry real risks. They hallucinate — confidently producing false statements and fabricated references — and they raise plagiarism and copyright questions. Journals have already retracted papers over AI-generated, nonsensical figures, and publishers (a BMJ spokesperson among them) have warned that AI is fueling "paper mills." Do not over-trust the output; treat it as a fast but unreliable assistant that you are responsible for.

The rules differ per venue and shift over time. What was disallowed two years ago may be permitted now — and vice versa. Guidance that stood in 2023 had changed by 2025. Some venues require you to pledge that no LLM was used; others require you to disclose exactly where AI was used (drafting, coding, brainstorming). Always read the current Call for Papers for the exact venue and year, and stay strictly within the allowed range — that responsibility is yours.

Recommended practice

  • Use LLMs minimally and as a supplement. Write your own draft, ask the model to check or refine, then take it to your advisor — the human review is the one that counts.
  • Verify everything. You are accountable for every claim, citation, and line of code the model touched.
  • Writing the method yourself has value beyond the text: the act of writing often changes how you think about the work, and outsourcing it can quietly cost you that insight.
  • Where a venue permits AI for a specific purpose — grammar correction, coding help, brainstorming — that is usually fine if you disclose it as required and stand behind the result.
Peer review is far stricter than authoring. Many venues forbid LLM use in reviewing altogether: a manuscript you were asked to review is unpublished and confidential, so pasting it into a model can be a copyright violation. IEEE permits LLMs for writing but not for peer review; NeurIPS likewise restricts reviewer use. When in doubt about reviewing, assume it is not allowed.

13 Conference vs Journal

Two publication venues with very different rhythms, expectations, and career weight.

A conference proceedings is the archive of the papers presented at a conference — the collected, published record of that event. Conferences run on hard deadlines because there is a fixed presentation schedule, and if your paper is accepted you present it. A journal works differently: it runs on iterative peer review, usually has no deadline, and accepts a paper once it has been revised enough. Journal papers are typically complete stories; conference papers are often an intermediate checkpoint of ongoing work.

AspectConferenceJournal
ArchivingCollected in the proceedingsPublished in journal issues
DeadlinesHard deadlines (fixed presentation schedule)Usually none
ReviewRound(s) tied to the scheduleIterative peer review until sufficiently revised
MaturityOften a checkpoint of ongoing workUsually a complete piece of work
TimelineWeeks to a few monthsCan take years (sometimes ~5)
PresentationYou present if acceptedNo presentation

Concretely, a journal paper tells the finished story — technique A had a weakness, so we built technique B, and here is exactly how it was evaluated — while a conference paper often reports solid but still-evolving progress.

Field changes everything. In computer science, conferences count as real publications and full career credit — sometimes scored higher than journals, effectively at journal level. In many other fields, a conference paper is regarded as intermediate and may not count as a publication at all. Know how your field and your evaluators weigh each venue before you choose where to submit.

Cover letter & response letter

When submitting — especially to journals — you write a cover letter stating why the paper matters and why it belongs in this particular venue. After a review round, you write a response letter that walks through each reviewer comment and explains precisely how you addressed it. Both are short but consequential: they frame how editors and reviewers read your work.

Template — a short journal cover letter

Dear Editor name,

We submit our manuscript “title” for consideration as a article type in journal.

The paper addresses problem. Existing approaches limitation. We propose idea, and show that main result.

We believe the work fits journal because connection to the journal’s scope or recent papers.

This manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors have approved the submission.

Sincerely, corresponding author

Before You Submit

A last pass, in the order reviewers will read the paper.

  • The title names the problem and the idea, not just the field.
  • The Abstract has a number in it, and every claim in it appears again in the paper.
  • Each item in the contributions list points to the section that delivers it.
  • Every figure and table is referenced in the text, and its caption makes sense on its own.
  • Figures stay readable in grayscale and at printed size.
  • Baselines are run under the same conditions as your method, and the setup is complete enough to reproduce.
  • Limitations are stated plainly — in the Conclusion, or wherever the venue requires.
  • All references come from the .bib file and are cited at least once; no placeholder “[?]” remains.
  • The paper is within the page limit using the official template, unmodified.
  • The current year's Call for Papers has been re-read: anonymity, LLM disclosure, supplementary material.
