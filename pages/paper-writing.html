Engineering Paper Writing Method Guide

A section-by-section field guide to writing engineering research papers — not about ideas, but about the act of writing the paper itself. However strong your method is, if the paper is poorly written it will not be accepted. This guide follows the real flow: understand the structure, outline first, then draft each section on purpose.

Distilled and translated into English from a graduate-level paper-writing special lecture (소휘수 교수님 논문 특강). The lecture used the paper “CHITIN: A Comprehensive In-thread Instruction Replication Technique Against Transient Faults” as its running example.

01 The Big Picture

What a paper actually is, where it starts, and how the pieces fit together.

A paper is a shared record of research

A paper is the process of sharing your research so that other people can see and build on it. Concretely, it is an experimental argument: you take an existing technique and the technique you built, compare them, and openly show which is better and why. Being able to find a worthwhile problem in the first place is itself the mark of a mature researcher — the whole of a master's or PhD is the practice of finding and solving problems.

The goal of this guide is narrow but critical: how to write the paper. Acceptance depends on it. No matter how good the method is, a paper that is written badly does not get accepted.

Don't start by writing — start by outlining

Never write a paper by diving straight into full prose. First outline: lay out, in bullets, why each piece belongs and how the argument flows. Only once the structure holds up do you expand bullets into paragraphs. A useful classroom exercise is to work in reverse — take a finished paper and write out the phrase/bullet behind each sentence — because seeing the skeleton teaches you the shape.

The anatomy of a paper

The content you truly write lives in Sections 1–5. In LaTeX the reference list is generated automatically, so learn that tooling well and spend your energy on the sections. The classic flow of the argument is:

A problem to solve ▼ Prior techniques that addressed it ▼ The limitation those techniques share ▼ Therefore, we propose this new technique
Two summaries, nested: the Introduction is a summary of the whole paper, and the Abstract is a summary of the Introduction. Everything except Abstract and Introduction maps roughly one-to-one onto the work you did.

After writing: the review process

Submitting is not the end. Understand the difference between a conference and a journal (see Section 9), and when reviews come back, revise and write a response letter explaining how you addressed each comment. For a conference, an accepted paper also means you present it.

02 Outlining → Drafting

The single most leveraged habit in paper writing.

“Outlining is the phase when you write in bullets. You do not need complete sentences — just phrases. Fix the structure of the paper, the sections and subsections, but inside them only bullets. Your arguments, and the flow of the arguments, are the main thing you are trying to fix. Once you have an outline, you iterate on it until the story is set. It is much easier to iterate on an outline than on writing, because (i) it is smaller, so you can see and think about all of it at once, and (ii) fixing it is much easier than fixing prose.”

The expansion ladder

Main bullet → Sub-bullet → Paragraph

Write each section as a main bullet, break it into sub-bullets for detail, then expand each into prose. When outlining is done, drafting follows almost mechanically — the hard thinking is already finished. Fixing a small outline is cheap; fixing 8 pages of prose is not.

Lead with the point. Writing top-down — state the key claim first, then add support — reads well and keeps reviewers oriented. If a paper deliberately breaks the standard structure, there is usually a reason; learning to spot that reason is itself valuable.

03 Introduction

The most important — and the hardest — section to write.

Every paper's Introduction looks broadly similar (other sections vary more from paper to paper). Its real job is to convince reviewers of how important the work is and how significant a contribution it makes. Reviewers judge from the Abstract and Introduction first; if there is nothing compelling there, they may not read further. First impressions decide.

Write for non-experts

A good paper can be read by an undergraduate, not only a specialist. Provide the background knowledge a reader without deep domain expertise needs. A rough recipe for the opening:

  • (A) General background — the broad context of the area.
  • (C) Field-specific terms — define the vocabulary the paper relies on. If a term is now common (e.g. "data mining" today), you can skip the definition and simply cite prior work; in 1996 it needed explaining.
  • (B) The problem with existing / general approaches — what is missing or broken.

From existing methods to your contribution

If existing solutions have no problem, there is no reason to propose a new one. So: briefly mention and summarize prior techniques (briefly — this is the Intro), classify them, then critique their common limitation. The pattern is: a shared limitation → we solved that shared limitation → and performance improved. Stating "this capability did not exist before, so we built it" is another good framing. Bold the sentences that name each limitation and you will see the skeleton of the argument.

Anticipate the reviewer. Writing these limitation/contribution sentences carefully — precisely and fairly — is what reduces reviewer pushback later.

What the Introduction should contain

  1. Background and field terms for a general reader.
  2. Existing approaches and their shared limitation.
  3. A short, high-level statement of your proposed method (what it is, in one or two sentences).
  4. Your contributions and their effect.
  5. A brief mention of results / conclusions.
  6. (Optional) a paper-structure paragraph — include only if space allows.

Build the skeleton first, then flesh it out during outlining: each of A–F becomes a sub-bullet, then a paragraph. The Introduction is genuinely the hardest section — expect to iterate.

04 Related Work & Citations

Establishing credibility — and where your work sits in the literature.

What Related Work does

Related Work summarizes prior research on the same problem, then organizes and states its limitations. Think of it as expanding into full paragraphs what the Introduction covered in a sentence or two. Do not attack prior work too aggressively; where useful, explain the context your paper emerged from. Showing the limitations clearly is what makes it obvious why your work was needed, and it can indirectly emphasize the necessity of your contribution. This section may also cover background a reader needs to follow the paper.

A weak survey can be an outright reject reason. Reviewers use Related Work to judge whether you actually know the field. A common structure: a table classifying prior methods, then subsections that explain each category briefly and pivot to its limitation (often with a "However, …"). To state a limitation you must first describe the approach at least roughly.

Practical structure

  • Introduce a category, then use However to turn to its limitation.
  • Lead with the key concept, then elaborate (top-down).
  • Write your own research category in more detail than the others.
  • Figures comparing an existing method vs. your method can cut length while improving clarity — a strict page budget (e.g. a 6-page conference paper) forces this. Adding a figure is often easier than rewriting text.
  • If you deliberately omit something, you can say why.

When there is no Related Work section

Sometimes Related Work is merged into the Introduction (keeping only a Preliminaries paragraph), usually because of page limits — e.g. the DATE conference caps papers at 6 pages. Some venues also push Related Work to the end of the paper. Each choice has trade-offs; when you are new, follow the standard structure, and deviate only with a clear purpose.

References & citation

A reference is an idea taken from prior work — cite as much as you legitimately can. Citing meaningful papers gives credit, and citation counts are a rough measure of impact (a landmark paper may be cited hundreds of thousands of times). Journals track an impact factor derived from references; conferences use other metrics. In computer science, top conferences are often treated at journal level.

Paraphrase — never copy-paste. Restate prior work in your own words and cite it. Do not reproduce sentences verbatim; summarize or rephrase — but keep technical terms unchanged. Careless referencing invites plagiarism concerns. Use citations to sharpen the contrast with prior work and to underline that the problem you tackle is serious.

Finding and organizing the literature

  • Citation chaining — follow a paper's references backward and its citing papers forward. Especially useful at the start of a project.
  • Survey papers — an authoritative survey is a map of a field. They are long (70–80 pages is normal) and heavily cited, so paraphrasing is mandatory.
  • Google Scholar shows which papers cite a given work — a fast way to move forward in time.
  • Collect candidate papers into a list first, then organize; some papers even publish their search strategy, which is worth studying.
  • Manage citation position carefully (e.g. when [2] and [3] would otherwise be ambiguous). In LaTeX, citation formatting is automatic; Korean-language venues written in HWP require manual formatting per style.

05 Method

Comparatively the easiest section — if you can explain what you built.

The Method section is the record of "found a problem → built a solution." If you can clearly explain your technique, you can write it. Depending on the technique, describe how it was designed and how it works.

Structure

  • Give an overview first, then break the details into subsections (a per-subsection figure helps a lot).
  • Explain, overall, how you solve the problem, what differs from prior methods, and by how much you improve on them.
  • The sentence just before a subsection should signal what that subsection will do (top-down). Keeping a consistent order makes the reader's job easier.
Figure-first. If you're stuck on the outline, draw the figure first. Once you have a clear overview diagram of how the system works, the explanation — and then the text — follows naturally. A single well-drawn overview figure can be reused throughout the paper. A common device: show the flaw in the prior approach and how your system fixes it, then write the paragraph that explains the figure.

Before writing, read a few papers close to yours and study how they are organized — prefer published papers over arXiv preprints, since preprints may not have passed review. In the end, the only job is to explain your technique effectively.

06 Experiments

Setup, results, analysis, contribution — and reproducibility above all.

The Experiments section states the environment and method, the results, and the analysis: How was the experiment set up? What results came out? How were they analyzed? How much did the work contribute? Describe the setup so precisely that someone else could reproduce it, and explain the performance metrics standard in your field.

More experiments is better. Thin evaluation draws "insufficient experiments" from reviewers; some venues (e.g. DAC) accept more readily when experiments are extensive — provided you have the compute. Additional and controlled experiments back up your claims and leave reviewers little to push on.

Reproducibility

  • Report the experimental environment, release code and results, disclose the models used, epochs, and how training was done.
  • If a baseline's code is not public, you may have to re-implement it from the paper. Match the environment as closely as possible — if the original ran on an RTX 3060 and you use an RTX 5090, hardware differences must be accounted for.
  • Many papers now open-source everything; if not, everything must be reconstructed faithfully from the text.

Presenting results

  • Present results mostly as graphs and tables, emphasizing your result clearly.
  • Defend the cases where you do well and where you do not — remember the term ablation study (systematically removing components to show each one's effect).
  • For a genuinely new problem there may be no existing baseline; explain more, or construct a reasonable baseline yourself.
  • If you want to make a difference more visible, present it from another angle — e.g. two paths that look identical in 2D may differ greatly in 3D.
Avoid default charts. Effort shows. Make series distinguishable even when printed in black & white; plain red-vs-blue can be indistinguishable to some readers and in grayscale. Draw clearly.

07 Conclusion

The most formulaic section — which makes it the easiest.

Conclusions are highly standardized; you can follow the usual template safely. The core is three things:

  1. An overall summary of the work.
  2. The limitations of your approach.
  3. Future work — e.g. "it would be worth trying this in another domain."
Some venues are explicit about this — NeurIPS, for instance, asks authors to state limitations. Cover summary, limitations, and future directions and reviewers rarely push back on the Conclusion.

08 Using LLMs Responsibly

Powerful help — under rules that vary by venue and change every year.

LLMs can turn bullets into paragraphs and help you brainstorm, but they carry real risks: hallucination, and potential plagiarism / copyright issues. There have already been retractions over AI-generated, nonsensical figures, and publishers have warned that AI is enabling "paper mills." Do not over-trust the output.

Rules differ per venue and change over time. What was disallowed two years ago may be allowed now (and vice-versa). Always check the current Call for Papers. Some venues require you to declare that no LLM was used; others require you to disclose exactly where AI was used (drafting, coding, brainstorming). Staying within the allowed range is your responsibility.

Recommended practice

  • Use LLMs minimally and as a supplement — write your own draft, ask the model to check or refine, then take it to your advisor.
  • Take responsibility for and verify everything the model produces.
  • Writing the method yourself matters: the act of writing can change how you think about the work.
  • Disclose usage when the venue requires it, and keep it honest and complete.
Peer review is stricter. Many venues forbid LLM use in reviewing entirely — an unpublished manuscript you were asked to review is confidential, so pasting it into a model can be a copyright violation. IEEE allows LLMs for writing but not for peer review; NeurIPS also restricts reviewer use.

09 Conference vs Journal

Two publication venues with very different rhythms.

AspectConferenceJournal
ArchivingPapers are collected in the proceedingsPublished in journal issues
DeadlinesHard deadlines (a fixed presentation schedule)Usually no deadline
ReviewRounds tied to the scheduleIterative peer review; accepted once sufficiently revised
MaturityOften a "checkpoint" of ongoing workUsually a complete piece of work
TimelineWeeks to monthsCan take years (sometimes ~5)
PresentationYou present if acceptedNo presentation

A journal paper is typically the finished story — technique A had a weakness, so we built technique B, and here is exactly how it was evaluated. A conference paper is often an intermediate checkpoint.

Field matters. In computer science, conferences count as real publications and career credit — sometimes scored higher than journals, essentially at journal level. In many other fields, conference papers are seen as intermediate and may not count as publications.

Cover letter

When submitting (especially to journals), a cover letter states why the paper matters and why it belongs in this venue. After revisions, a response letter explains how each reviewer comment was addressed.
