Engineering Paper Writing Method Guide

A section-by-section field guide to writing engineering research papers — not about generating ideas, but about the act of writing the paper itself. This is the part most people underestimate: however strong your method is, a paper that is written badly does not get accepted. Acceptance is a writing problem as much as a research problem.

The guide follows the real order of work — understand the structure, outline in bullets, iterate on the outline, and only then draft each section on purpose, section by section, the way an experienced writer actually does it.

Distilled and translated into English from a graduate-level paper-writing special lecture (소휘수 교수님 논문 특강). The lecture used the paper “CHITIN: A Comprehensive In-thread Instruction Replication Technique Against Transient Faults” (a software fault-tolerance paper built on the SRMT idea) as its running example.

01 The Big Picture

What a paper actually is, where it begins, and how the pieces fit together before you write a single sentence.

A paper is a shared record of research

A paper is the process of sharing your research so other people can see it, trust it, and build on it. Stripped down, it is an experimental argument: you take an existing technique and the technique you built, compare them under fair conditions, and openly show which is better and why. That act of comparing and disclosing is the paper.

Before any of that, though, comes the hardest skill: finding a problem worth solving. Being able to locate a real, tractable problem in the first place is what distinguishes a mature researcher — the entire arc of a master's or PhD is repeated practice at finding and solving problems. This guide assumes you have a problem and a method, and focuses on the step people neglect: turning that work into an accepted paper.

The scope of this guide is deliberately narrow but decisive: how to write the paper. No matter how good the method is, if you cannot write it well, it will not be accepted — and an unaccepted result may as well not exist to the community. (Note: this lecture does not cover writing survey papers, which are a different genre with their own conventions.)

Don't start by writing — start by outlining

Never write a paper by opening a blank file and typing full prose from the top. First outline: lay out, in bullets, why each piece belongs and how the argument flows. Fix the structure — sections, subsections, the order of claims — while it is still cheap to move things around. Only once the skeleton holds up do you expand bullets into paragraphs.

A powerful way to learn this is to work in reverse: take a finished, published paper and, sentence by sentence, write down the phrase or bullet that each sentence came from. Reconstructing the skeleton of a good paper teaches you the shape you are aiming for — and it is a genuinely useful exercise in its own right.

The anatomy of a paper

The content you truly author lives in Sections 1–5 (Introduction, Related Work, Method, Experiments, Conclusion). The reference list, by contrast, is generated automatically in LaTeX — so learn that tooling once and spend your real effort on the sections. If you can carry an argument through the standard flow below, your draft already has the shape of a paper:

A problem that needs solving ▼ Prior techniques that tried to address it ▼ The limitation those techniques share ▼ Therefore, we propose this new technique ▼ …and we show, experimentally, that it is better

In the running example, the CHITIN paper follows exactly this spine: hardware is vulnerable to transient faults → prior hardware/software approaches exist → they share limitations (cost, coverage) → so we propose a software instruction-replication technique → and we demonstrate its coverage and overhead.

Two summaries, nested. The Introduction is a summary of the whole paper, and the Abstract is a summary of the Introduction. Everything except the Abstract and Introduction maps roughly one-to-one onto the work you actually did — which is why those two are the hardest and the most read.

Writing is not the end — the review loop is

Submitting is a milestone, not the finish line. Understand the difference between a conference and a journal (Section 9), because it changes deadlines, expectations, and what "done" means. When reviews come back, you revise and write a response letter that explains, point by point, how you addressed each comment. For a conference, acceptance also means you present the work. Writing well up front is what makes this loop short instead of brutal.

02 Outlining → Drafting

The single most leveraged habit in paper writing. Master this and every section gets easier.

“Outlining is the phase when you write in bullets. You do not need complete sentences — just phrases. Try to fix the structure of the paper: write down the sections and subsections, but inside them there are no sentences, just bullets. Your arguments, and the flow of the arguments, are the main thing you are trying to fix. Once you have a version of the outline, you iterate on it and refine it until the story is set. It is much easier to iterate on an outline than on writing, because (i) the outline is smaller, so you can see and think about all of it at once, and (ii) fixing it is much easier than fixing writing.”
— MPS Lab, “How to Write a Paper”

The expansion ladder

Main bullet → Sub-bullets → Paragraph

Write each section as a single main bullet (its job in one line). Break that into sub-bullets that carry the detail, the sub-claims, and the evidence. Then expand each sub-bullet into prose. By the time you reach prose, the hard thinking — what to say, in what order, and why — is already done, so drafting becomes almost mechanical.

The reason this works is economic. Fixing a ten-line outline is trivial; restructuring eight pages of finished prose is painful and error-prone. Every structural problem you catch at the outline stage is a problem you never have to untangle later.

Lead with the point (top-down). State the key claim first, then add support. Reviewers stay oriented, and each paragraph announces its purpose before defending it. Professors who write this way — stating the important concept up front, then elaborating — are consistently easier to read.
When a paper breaks the standard structure, it is usually on purpose. Page limits, a nonstandard contribution, or a venue's house style can all justify deviation. Early on, follow the canonical structure; later, learn to read why an author departed from it — that reading skill is part of becoming a writer.

03 Introduction

The most important — and the hardest — section to write. Budget the most iterations here.

Every paper's Introduction looks broadly similar (the later sections vary far more from paper to paper), because its job is always the same: convince the reviewers of how important the problem is and how significant your contribution is. Reviewers judge from the Abstract and Introduction first; if there is nothing compelling there, many will not read on. Today some readers even paste papers into an LLM for a summary — but that summary is still built from your Intro. First impressions decide, so a good Introduction is written for a broad audience: an undergraduate, not only a specialist, should be able to follow it.

The opening: give a non-expert the background

Provide the background a reader without deep domain expertise needs. A reliable recipe for the opening blends three ingredients:

  • (A) General background — the broad context and motivation of the area.
  • (C) Field-specific terms — define the vocabulary the paper depends on. Calibrate to the era and audience: a term that needed explaining years ago may now be assumed. In 1996 you had to define "data mining"; today you simply cite prior work and move on. Explaining a now-common keyword wastes space.
  • (B) The problem with existing / general approaches — what is missing, broken, or insufficient about how the field currently handles this.

Explaining the general research setting is itself part of the background. The goal is that a reader arrives at your contribution already understanding why it is needed.

From existing methods to your contribution

If existing solutions had no problem, there would be no reason to propose a new one — so the Introduction has to establish that a gap exists. The move is: briefly mention and summarize prior techniques (briefly — this is the Intro, not Related Work), classify them, then critique their shared limitation. The rhetorical pattern is:

Existing methods, summarized ▼ Their common limitation ▼ We solve that common limitation ▼ …and performance improves

An equally good framing is the "gap" version: "this capability simply did not exist before, so we built it." Both end in the same place — a clearly stated contribution that the reviewer can see is new.

Skeleton trick: take a strong Introduction and bold only the sentences that name a limitation of prior work. What remains in bold is the argument's skeleton — a clean chain of "this was missing → we address it." Write your own Intro so that this skeleton would be equally visible.
Anticipate the reviewer. The limitation and contribution sentences are where reviewers push back hardest. Writing them precisely and fairly — not overclaiming, not attacking — is exactly what reduces pushback later. A sentence that overstates your novelty is an invitation for a reviewer to find a counterexample.

What the Introduction should contain

  1. Background and field terms, pitched for a general reader (A + C).
  2. Existing approaches and the limitation they share (B).
  3. A short, high-level statement of your proposed method — what it is, in one or two sentences (not the mechanism, just the idea).
  4. Your contributions and their effect (e.g., "our decoding scheme hides the loss of an entire frame").
  5. A brief mention of results / conclusions to signal that the claims are backed up.
  6. (Optional) a paper-structure paragraph ("Section 2 reviews… Section 3 presents…") — include only if space allows; drop it first when you are over the page budget.

Build this skeleton first, then flesh it out during outlining: each of items 1–6 becomes a sub-bullet, then a paragraph. Expect the Introduction to take the most drafts of any section — it genuinely is the hardest, and that is normal.

04 Related Work & Citations

Where you prove you know the field — and place your work within it. A weak survey here can sink an otherwise good paper.

What Related Work does

Related Work surveys prior research on the same problem, organizes it, and states its limitations. Think of it as expanding into full paragraphs what the Introduction covered in a sentence or two. It shows the reviewer that you genuinely know the literature, and by making prior limitations explicit it makes it obvious why your work was necessary — which indirectly strengthens the case for your contribution. It is also where you can supply the background a reader needs to follow the rest of the paper.

Tone matters: do not attack prior work too aggressively. Where useful, explain the context your paper grew out of, and acknowledge what earlier methods got right before turning to what they missed.

A poor survey can be an outright reject reason. Reviewers use Related Work to test whether you actually command the field. If key prior work is missing, expect comments like "the authors appear unaware of [X]" — and that alone can end a submission. A thorough, fair survey is table stakes for credibility.

Practical structure

  • Open with a table classifying prior methods into categories, then give each category a subsection.
  • Within a subsection, describe the approach briefly, then pivot to its limitation — often with a "However, …". You must describe an approach at least roughly before you are entitled to criticize it.
  • Lead with the key concept, then elaborate (top-down), the same discipline as everywhere else.
  • Write your own research category in more depth than the others — that is the neighborhood your contribution lives in, so readers need it in sharper focus.
  • A figure comparing an existing method against your method can cut length while improving clarity. Under a strict page budget (e.g., a 6-page conference paper) this is often necessary — and adding a figure is usually easier than rewriting paragraphs.
  • If you deliberately omit a line of work, you can say so and why.
  • When your paper builds directly on one concept (in the example, SRMT), give that concept more room — sometimes even its own section — so its variants and your relationship to them are unmistakable. Skipping this invites "how does this differ from SRMT variant Y?" pushback.

When there is no Related Work section

Sometimes Related Work is folded into the Introduction, leaving only a short Preliminaries paragraph — usually to satisfy a page limit (the DATE conference, for instance, caps papers at 6 pages; a comparison table may even be pushed into the Intro). Some venues instead move Related Work to the end of the paper so the method comes first. Each choice trades off reader guidance against momentum. When you are new, follow the standard placement; deviate only with a clear reason.

References & citation: why they matter

A reference is an idea you took from prior work — so cite as much as you legitimately can. Citing meaningful papers gives proper credit, and citation counts are a rough proxy for impact: a landmark paper may accumulate hundreds of thousands of citations (some exceed 200k), and that reach is what "high impact" means. Journals summarize this as an impact factor derived from references; it is an imperfect, sometimes controversial metric. Conferences are judged differently — and in computer science, top conferences are frequently treated at journal level.

Field note (CS): in computer science, a strong conference paper is real career credit and can be rated above a journal. Community-curated venue tiers (e.g., the Pusnow ranking list) are a common reference for where a venue sits. In LaTeX, citation formatting is automatic; Korean-language venues written in HWP require formatting each entry by hand, per the target style.

Paraphrase — never copy-paste

You may quote directly on occasion, but the default is to restate prior work in your own words and cite it. Never reproduce sentences verbatim — summarize or rephrase — but keep technical terms unchanged (don't "creatively" rename an established concept). Careless referencing invites plagiarism concerns; this matters doubly for surveys, which are citation-dense and must be paraphrased throughout.

Copying (don't)

"SRMT duplicates instructions within a single thread and compares the results to detect transient faults." — lifted word-for-word from the source.

Paraphrase + cite (do)

"Prior in-thread replication schemes such as SRMT re-execute instructions inside one thread and cross-check the outputs, catching transient faults without extra hardware [3]."

Finding and organizing the literature

  • Citation chaining — walk a paper's references backward and its citing papers forward. Especially valuable at the start of a project for mapping a field quickly.
  • Google Scholar shows which papers cite a given work — a fast way to move forward in time and to find the papers whose claims support yours (paraphrase those to strengthen your own argument).
  • Survey papers are field maps and excellent guideposts when entering a new area. They are authoritative but long (70–80 pages is normal) and heavily cited — read them for orientation, and never copy from them.
  • Collect candidate papers into a running list first, then organize and classify. Some papers even publish their literature search strategy; studying those teaches you how to search well yourself.
  • Watch citation position so markers don't blur together — e.g., when [2] and [3] would otherwise be ambiguous, place each next to the claim it supports.

05 Method

Comparatively the easiest section to write — if you can clearly explain what you built.

The Method section is the record of "found a problem → built a solution." If you can explain your technique clearly, you can write this section. Depending on the technique, describe how it was designed and how it operates, in enough detail that a competent reader could reconstruct it.

Structure

  • Give an overview first — a single diagram or paragraph showing how the whole system works — then break the details into subsections, ideally one figure per subsection.
  • Cover three things overall: how you solve the problem, what differs from prior methods, and by how much you improve on them.
  • The sentence immediately before a subsection should announce what that subsection will do (top-down). Keeping a consistent order across subsections makes the reader's job dramatically easier.
  • When a point is central, it is fine to spend more space on it — and sometimes to pull "why this matters" into its own short section.
Figure-first. If you're stuck on the outline, draw the figure first. Once you have a clear overview diagram of how the system works, the explanation — and then the text — follows naturally. A single well-drawn overview figure can be reused throughout the paper. A common and effective device: draw the flaw in the prior approach beside how your system fixes it, then write the paragraph that simply explains the figure.
Study the neighbors first. Before writing, read a few papers close to yours and observe how they are organized — section order, what goes in a figure vs. text, how much math. Prefer published papers over arXiv preprints, since a preprint may not have passed review and may model habits you don't want to copy. In the end, the only job of this section is to explain your technique effectively.

06 Experiments

Setup, results, analysis, contribution — and, above everything, reproducibility.

The Experiments section answers four questions: How was the experiment set up? What results came out? How were they analyzed? How much did the work contribute? Describe the environment and procedure precisely enough that someone else could rebuild it, and explain the performance metrics that are standard in your field (accuracy, latency, coverage, overhead — whatever your problem is measured by).

More experiments is (almost always) better. Thin evaluation reliably draws an "insufficient experiments" review. Some venues — DAC is a classic example — accept more readily when the evaluation is extensive, provided you have the compute to run it. Additional and controlled experiments back your claims and leave reviewers little to grab onto.

Reproducibility

  • Report the full experimental environment; where possible, open-source your code and results, and disclose the models used, the number of epochs, and how training was done. Many papers now release everything by default.
  • If a baseline's code is not public, you may have to re-implement it from the paper — and then match the environment as closely as possible. Hardware differences are real: if the original ran on an RTX 3060 and you run on an RTX 5090, raw timings are not comparable, so you must control for that (same hardware, or normalized reporting).
  • The reason for all of this is that results others cannot reproduce are results reviewers cannot trust.

Presenting and defending results

  • Present results mostly as graphs and tables, emphasizing your result clearly.
  • Run an ablation study — systematically remove one component at a time to show what each contributes. (Memorize this term; reviewers expect it.) It is how you prove that each part of your method earns its place.
  • Defend the cases where you do well and where you do not. Honestly addressing weak spots pre-empts the reviewer who would otherwise raise them.
  • For a genuinely new problem there may be no existing baseline; then explain more carefully, or construct a reasonable baseline yourself to compare against.
  • Add experiments that back the theory from multiple angles. If a difference looks small from one view, show it from another — e.g., two trajectories that look identical in 2D can differ dramatically when plotted in 3D.
Avoid default charts. Effort is visible, and so is its absence. Make series distinguishable even in black & white (papers are often printed grayscale): use distinct markers, line styles, and hatching — not just a red line vs. a blue line, which can be indistinguishable to colorblind readers and in grayscale. A clear, deliberate figure reads as a careful paper.

07 Conclusion

The most formulaic section — which is exactly why it is the easiest.

Conclusions are highly standardized in both structure and phrasing, so you can safely follow the usual template. Read a few conclusions in your target venue and you will see the same shape repeat. The core is three moves:

  1. An overall summary — restate the problem, the method, and the headline result in compact form.
  2. The limitations of your approach — stated plainly and without defensiveness.
  3. Future work — where this could go next; "it would be worth trying this in another domain" is a perfectly good, common form.
Some venues require limitations explicitly. NeurIPS, for example, asks authors to state the limitations of their work. Cover summary, limitations, and future directions and reviewers very rarely push back on the Conclusion — it is the section least likely to cost you an acceptance, so don't overthink it.

08 Using LLMs Responsibly

Genuinely useful help — under rules that vary by venue and change every single year.

LLMs can turn a set of bullets into draft paragraphs and help you brainstorm framings, and it is increasingly unrealistic to avoid them entirely. But they carry real risks. They hallucinate — confidently producing false statements and fabricated references — and they raise plagiarism and copyright questions. Journals have already retracted papers over AI-generated, nonsensical figures, and publishers (a BMJ spokesperson among them) have warned that AI is fueling "paper mills." Do not over-trust the output; treat it as a fast but unreliable assistant that you are responsible for.

The rules differ per venue and shift over time. What was disallowed two years ago may be permitted now — and vice versa. Guidance that stood in 2023 had changed by 2025. Some venues require you to pledge that no LLM was used; others require you to disclose exactly where AI was used (drafting, coding, brainstorming). Always read the current Call for Papers for the exact venue and year, and stay strictly within the allowed range — that responsibility is yours.

Recommended practice

  • Use LLMs minimally and as a supplement. Write your own draft, ask the model to check or refine, then take it to your advisor — the human review is the one that counts.
  • Verify everything. You are accountable for every claim, citation, and line of code the model touched.
  • Writing the method yourself has value beyond the text: the act of writing often changes how you think about the work, and outsourcing it can quietly cost you that insight.
  • Where a venue permits AI for a specific purpose — grammar correction, coding help, brainstorming — that is usually fine if you disclose it as required and stand behind the result.
Peer review is far stricter than authoring. Many venues forbid LLM use in reviewing altogether: a manuscript you were asked to review is unpublished and confidential, so pasting it into a model can be a copyright violation. IEEE permits LLMs for writing but not for peer review; NeurIPS likewise restricts reviewer use. When in doubt about reviewing, assume it is not allowed.

09 Conference vs Journal

Two publication venues with very different rhythms, expectations, and career weight.

A conference proceedings is the archive of the papers presented at a conference — the collected, published record of that event. Conferences run on hard deadlines because there is a fixed presentation schedule, and if your paper is accepted you present it. A journal works differently: it runs on iterative peer review, usually has no deadline, and accepts a paper once it has been revised enough. Journal papers are typically complete stories; conference papers are often an intermediate checkpoint of ongoing work.

AspectConferenceJournal
ArchivingCollected in the proceedingsPublished in journal issues
DeadlinesHard deadlines (fixed presentation schedule)Usually none
ReviewRound(s) tied to the scheduleIterative peer review until sufficiently revised
MaturityOften a checkpoint of ongoing workUsually a complete piece of work
TimelineWeeks to a few monthsCan take years (sometimes ~5)
PresentationYou present if acceptedNo presentation

Concretely, a journal paper tells the finished story — technique A had a weakness, so we built technique B, and here is exactly how it was evaluated — while a conference paper often reports solid but still-evolving progress.

Field changes everything. In computer science, conferences count as real publications and full career credit — sometimes scored higher than journals, effectively at journal level. In many other fields, a conference paper is regarded as intermediate and may not count as a publication at all. Know how your field and your evaluators weigh each venue before you choose where to submit.

Cover letter & response letter

When submitting — especially to journals — you write a cover letter stating why the paper matters and why it belongs in this particular venue. After a review round, you write a response letter that walks through each reviewer comment and explains precisely how you addressed it. Both are short but consequential: they frame how editors and reviewers read your work.
